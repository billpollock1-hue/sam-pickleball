How Much Does A Game's Score Affect My Rating?

Two Things Multiply Together, Every Time
The Question

A Score Isn't Just Win Or Lose

Two games can have the same winner and still move ratings by very different amounts. Win 11-10 and your rating barely nudges. Win 11-0 in the same matchup and it moves noticeably more. That's not a quirk — it's the whole point of using score margin at all: a narrow win is weaker evidence of a skill gap than a lopsided one, and the rating math is built to treat it that way.

The Mechanism

Two Things Multiply Together

Every game moves your rating by two things multiplied together: how surprising the result was, and how big the score margin was.

Surprise is the gap between what actually happened and what your ratings predicted. Beat someone you were expected to beat and there's not much surprise; upset a much higher-rated opponent and there's a lot. Margin is the score gap itself, run through a natural log so that going from a 1-point win to a 2-point win matters more than going from a 9-point win to a 10-point win — each additional point of margin adds a little less than the one before it.

rating change  =  K × (actual result − expected result) × ln(point difference + 1)

K is a fixed scale, not a third factor being multiplied in for effect — it just sets how big a maximal swing can be. It's 20 for established players and eases down from 40 over a new player's first 60 games while the system is still learning where they belong. Everything below uses the steady-state value, K = 20.

Example 1

Evenly Matched Teams — Score Is Everything

When both sides are rated about the same, the surprise term is fixed at its midpoint — a coin-flip result was expected either way — so margin alone decides how much the rating moves. Here's the complete picture for every possible margin in a game to 11, between two evenly matched teams:

ScoreWinnerLoser
11–10+7−7
11–9+11−11
11–8+14−14
11–7+16−16
11–6+18−18
11–5+19−19
11–4+21−21
11–3+22−22
11–2+23−23
11–1+24−24
11–0+25−25

Notice the curve flattens as the blowout gets more lopsided: the jump from 11-10 to 11-9 is worth 4 points, but the jump from 11-1 to 11-0 is only worth 1. A log scale means the difference between a close game and a fairly close game matters a lot; the difference between a big win and a slightly bigger win doesn't matter nearly as much.

Example 2

Same Score, Different Surprise

Now hold the margin fixed and change how surprising the result is. Take two players 200 rating points apart — a real, established skill gap — and have them play the same 11-2 game:

+35
Underdog wins 11–2 (upset)
+11
Favorite wins 11–2 (expected)

Same score, three times the rating movement. The favorite winning 11-2 barely tells the system anything it didn't already expect. The underdog winning 11-2 is a real surprise, and the rating reacts accordingly — that's the surprise term doing its job, independent of margin.

Example 3

Same Underdog, Different Margin

Finally, hold the surprise fixed and change the margin. Same 200-point underdog from Example 2, winning by two different amounts:

+17
Underdog wins 11–9 (narrow)
+38
Underdog wins 11–0 (blowout)
Both wins are equally surprising — same rating gap, same upset. The blowout still counts for more than twice as much, because a shutout is much stronger evidence that the gap between these two players isn't what the ratings currently say.

The Short Version

Every rating change is surprise times margin, scaled by K.
How unexpected the result was, and how lopsided the score was — both matter, and neither one alone tells the whole story.

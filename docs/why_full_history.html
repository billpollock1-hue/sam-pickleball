Wouldn't 2025–2026 Be Enough?

Why Ratings Use Full History
The Question

A Fair Thing To Wonder

Today's ratings are built from every recorded game back to January 2022. Skill changes over years, though — people improve, people fade, the whole player pool turns over. So it's a completely reasonable question: wouldn't the numbers be a truer read on where everyone stands right now if we just drew a line at January 1, 2025 and rated everyone strictly on 2025–2026 play?

Short answer: no — and we recently got to test that almost exactly, by accident.

How We Measure It

A Rating Isn't A Lookup — It's A Running Number

Ratings don't work by storing every game you've ever played and averaging them. After each game, a player's rating simply moves: new rating = old rating + K × (what actually happened − what the model expected). New players move faster while the system is still learning where they belong (K starts at 40 and eases down over their first 60 games); everyone else moves at a steady K of 20 per game.

That mechanism has a quiet consequence: a game doesn't get "erased" by a calendar date, because it was never sitting there waiting to be looked up. Its effect on today's number has already been overwritten, partially, by every single game that happened after it. A result from 2022 has had thousands of updates layered on top of it since. Old data doesn't need a cutoff to stop mattering — it fades out on its own, continuously, the same way it has for chess ratings for the better part of a century.

The Proof

We Just Ran This Experiment

In September 2026 we found and corrected two real data errors — one from a single duplicated game pair on 2023-09-27, over three years old, and one from a systemic duplicate day on 2026-09-25. Fixing them meant recomputing every rating forward from the earliest error, 603 dates in a row, all the way to today.

Only 23 players ever saw a rating value shift at any point in that whole 603-date recompute, and the largest swing — even on the exact day of the fix — was 2 points. Every one of those nudges had washed out completely by August 2026. As of today, every single player's rating is bit-for-bit identical to what it would have been if that three-year-old error had never happened.

If data from years back still meaningfully drove today's ratings, correcting a real error that old should have moved something. It moved nothing, for anyone, as of today.
The Core Flaws

What A Hard 2025–2026 Cutoff Would Actually Do

THE COLD-START PROBLEM — the moment you draw a line at January 1, 2025, every player needs a starting rating for that day. Where does it come from? Either you carry forward their rating as of that date — which still encodes every game before it, the exact thing the cutoff was supposed to avoid — or you reset everyone to a common number and let the ratings re-establish from scratch, erasing real, earned skill separation overnight.
CLIFF EDGES, NOT TRENDS — with a rolling or fixed window, a rating can jump the moment an old game ages out, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything a player did recently. That's a harder thing to explain and trust than a number that only ever moves because of an actual result.
ALREADY TESTED, AND IT HURT ACCURACY — making ratings react more strongly to recent games isn't a new idea here; an earlier version of the system tried exactly that, weighting recent play more heavily. Tested directly against real results, it produced worse predictions overall and specific players with unstable, inflated ratings built on a handful of outlier games rather than a sustained record. Reverting to the steadier, full-history design fixed it.
What Already Handles This

Recency Matters — Just Not By Deleting Math

The concern underneath this question is usually really about inactive or long-gone players cluttering the standings, not about the rating math itself. That part is already handled separately: the leaderboard has its own recent-participation requirement, and players who've gone quiet long enough drop off the rankings entirely. Their number doesn't get deleted — it's just no longer shown as a current, ranked result. That keeps the leaderboard reflecting who's actually playing right now, without throwing away the accumulated signal that makes everyone else's rating trustworthy.

The Short Version

Ratings fade old results out automatically, every game, without a cutoff.
We tested it directly on a real three-year-old correction — it changed nothing today.
A hard window would trade that for cold starts and cliff edges, and we've already tried the more-reactive version and it made ratings worse.

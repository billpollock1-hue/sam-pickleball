Wouldn't 2025–2026 Be Enough?

Why Ratings Use Full History
The Question

A Fair Thing To Wonder

Today's ratings are built from every recorded game back to January 2022. Skill changes over years, though — people improve, people fade, the whole player pool turns over. So it's a completely reasonable question: wouldn't the numbers be a truer read on where everyone stands right now if we just drew a line at January 1, 2025 and rated everyone strictly on 2025–2026 play?

Short answer: no — and the reason isn't about picking a better line to draw. It's that the math already handles this, without needing one.

How We Measure It

A Rating Isn't A Lookup — It's A Running Number

Ratings don't work by storing every game you've ever played and averaging them. After each game, a player's rating simply moves: new rating = old rating + K × (what actually happened − what the model expected). New players move faster while the system is still learning where they belong (K starts at 40 and eases down over their first 60 games); everyone else moves at a steady K of 20 per game.

That mechanism has a quiet consequence: a game doesn't get "erased" by a calendar date, because it was never sitting there waiting to be looked up. Its effect on today's number has already been overwritten, partially, by every single game that happened after it. A result from 2022 has had thousands of updates layered on top of it since. Old data doesn't need a cutoff to stop mattering — it fades out on its own, continuously, the same way it has for chess ratings for the better part of a century.

Self-Correction, By Design

Every Game Re-Confirms The Rating

A rating only survives if it keeps being backed up by results. If a number ever drifts from what a player is actually capable of — a fluke game, a rough stretch, anything that doesn't reflect sustained play — every game after that acts as a fresh check against reality. If the rating doesn't match how the player is actually performing, the next results pull it back toward what's earned, the same way it always does. That correction doesn't have an expiration date; it works identically whether the mismatch happened last week or four years ago.

Genuine skill doesn't get corrected away the same way, because it keeps being reconfirmed by exactly the same process. A player who was genuinely better in 2023 and has stayed that way has had that fact re-validated by every match since. That's the real reason full history is safe to use: it's not that old results are ignored or forgotten, it's that anything in a rating that isn't backed by a real, sustained pattern gets self-corrected out, automatically, regardless of when it happened. A short window doesn't add that protection — it's already built in.

Tested, Not Just Argued

Checked Directly, At Realistic Window Lengths

This isn't just a design philosophy — it's been tested against real outcomes. Using every 2026 game where all four players had enough rated history to matter (2,301 games), we scored how well different rating designs actually predicted what happened on the court, using Brier score and log loss — the standard measures of how well a model's stated probabilities match reality. Lower is better for both.

The test compared full history against a genuinely hard cutoff — not a gradual fade, the literal thing a fixed window does: games older than the line count for exactly nothing — at the window lengths people actually propose.

At every window length from 12 to 48 months, full history matched or beat the windowed version — 18 months came out close enough to call a tie, and every other length tested came out worse for windowing, consistently, on both measures.

Windows under a year actually scored better on this test — but that's not a fair comparison. The test only scores a game if all four players already have a long track record, 60 or more rated games. With only a few months of games to draw from, most players never reach that many that fast, so far fewer games qualify to be scored at all (870 games at 6 months, versus 2,301 for full history). The games that do qualify under a short window are almost all between the club's most frequent players, since they're the only ones who rack up 60 games that quickly — a narrower, more predictable slice of the schedule than the real one. Short windows look good here for the wrong reason: not because they predict better, but because the hardest games to predict — the ones involving newer or less frequent players — get dropped from the test entirely.

Across every realistic length anyone has actually proposed, the data doesn't just fail to support a window — it favors full history outright.
The Core Flaws

What A Hard 2025–2026 Cutoff Would Actually Do

THE COLD-START PROBLEM — the moment you draw a line at January 1, 2025, every player needs a starting rating for that day. Where does it come from? Either you carry forward their rating as of that date — which still encodes every game before it, the exact thing the cutoff was supposed to avoid — or you reset everyone to a common number and let the ratings re-establish from scratch, erasing real, earned skill separation overnight.
A FIXED LINE DOESN'T STAY FIXED — draw the cutoff at January 1, 2025 and never move it again, and in a couple of years "2025 and 2026" is exactly as old and arbitrary as "since 2022" sounds today — the objection doesn't get solved, just relocated a little further down the calendar. The only way to keep a fixed window actually recent is to keep advancing the start date, and every time that happens, it's the cold-start problem all over again for every player at once. A rolling window doesn't avoid this either — it just spreads the same discontinuity into a small jump every single day, forever, as yesterday's oldest game falls out of range for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's actual play.
WEIGHTING RECENT GAMES MORE ISN'T FREE — the more heavily a rating system leans on recent results, the more it swings on small samples. A hot week or a cold one starts to look like a real change in skill, and the number gets noisier instead of more accurate. A rating built on a long, steady run of results is more trustworthy precisely because it isn't that reactive.
What Already Handles This

Recency Matters — Just Not By Deleting Math

The concern underneath this question is usually really about inactive or long-gone players cluttering the standings, not about the rating math itself. That part is already handled separately: the leaderboard has its own recent-participation requirement, and players who've gone quiet long enough drop off the rankings entirely. Their number doesn't get deleted — it's just no longer shown as a current, ranked result. That keeps the leaderboard reflecting who's actually playing right now, without throwing away the accumulated signal that makes everyone else's rating trustworthy.

The Short Version

A rating only holds if real results keep backing it up — anything that doesn't gets corrected out automatically, no matter how old it is.
A fixed or rolling cutoff doesn't add that protection; it just trades it for cold starts and an arbitrary line that ages just as fast as the one it replaced.
